Every feature

Everything Slate does, area by area, down to each setting and shortcut. Almost all of it is set from the Settings app, and every setting is searchable there.

 Slate is moving quickly, so this list may be a step behind the latest version.

 Session

Its own session

  • Installs as a Slate entry on any login screen; your existing desktop stays as it is
  • Runs with systemd, and without it where elogind is: Artix, Void and Gentoo. There, Slate shows you the command to switch a service on rather than doing it, and starts sound for the session itself
  • Runs Hyprland with Slate's own config, separate from ~/.config/hypr
  • Starts on a plain dark background that matches the login screen, without Hyprland's or your distro's default wallpaper flashing up first
  • Your own tweaks go in ~/.config/slate/hypr/ and are never overwritten
  • Slate restarts itself on its own code changes; Restart the shell in Settings → About
  • A welcome on first start: tiling or floating, colours from the wallpaper, glass or flat, theming your other apps, where the bar and dock go, and the shortcuts (with what the Super key is), each with a recommendation. It follows your theme, glass included, whenever you open it again

Password prompts

  • A built-in authentication agent, so admin prompts (pkexec, system settings) appear in Slate's style

 Menu bar

Placement

  • Top, bottom, left or right of the screen (moving it slides it off and back in at its new edge)
  • Auto-hide, revealed at the screen edge
  • With glass, a slider for how see-through the bar's background is, and a switch for the blur behind it
  • Sliders for how solid the containers on the bar are, and how solid they turn under the pointer (with glass)
  • The panels that open from the bar share its colour, with an opacity slider and a blur switch of their own (with glass)
  • Every button answers across the bar's full height (its full width down a side), not just on the shape drawn in the middle, so you can push the pointer against the screen's edge and click
  • Rest the pointer on anything on the bar and a small label says what it is (Wi-Fi, Clock, a tray app's name, which workspace a dot is), just below it, or beside it on a side bar
  • Flares into the screen with rounded corners
  • Dimmed on monitors you aren't using

Arrange it your way

  • Edit layout (Settings → Menu Bar, or right-click an empty part of the bar): the bar itself becomes the editor, with everything behind it dimmed
  • Right-click an empty part of the bar: Auto-hide, Position, Clock (12 or 24-hour, seconds), Edit layout and its settings
  • Drag anything along the bar: a gap opens where it will land and it follows your pointer, as on the dock
  • Containers hold things together: drag things in and out, move a whole one by its gripper, split one up, or add an empty one
  • Flexible spaces take up the spare room and push what's either side apart; add as many as you like (the bar always keeps one)
  • What you don't use waits in a pool under the bar; drag it back, or click it, when you want it
  • Click anything for its options: whether it shows its text, the clock's date and seconds, how a container collapses
  • The panel of the first or last button on the bar sits flush with that edge of the screen, wherever you put it
  • Defaults puts everything back as Slate first had it

When it doesn't all fit

  • On a smaller screen, a bigger Interface size or font, or with a long network name, the bar slims itself rather than running off the end: the window's title shortens and then goes, text beside icons goes (longest first), the clock sheds its date and seconds
  • Containers collapse Never, Always or When needed: When needed, how they start, tucks one behind its button only when the bar's short of room
  • What still doesn't fit waits behind More, a button you place like anything else: its panel lists each thing with its name and value, and a row does what the thing does on the bar
  • Menu item priority: Set priority, in Edit layout, opens a list you drag into order, what's at the top staying on the bar longest, beside a plain explanation of what the bar does when it's short of room
  • You choose which things always stay (Power, the clock and the control centre to start with); those never leave, so they're not in the list
  • Try it shorter, a menu in Edit layout, shows your bar at three quarters, half or a third of its length, in the middle of the screen's edge
  • Everything comes back, in reverse, as soon as there's room

Left side

  • Control centre button (the slate mark); right-click it for Settings, the wallpaper, Do Not Disturb, Night Light, glass, updates, restarting Slate, locking and logging out
  • Apps button, with right-click shortcuts to Settings pages
  • Spaces button: save and bring back sets of workspaces (see Windows)
  • Nova's button, while the assistant is switched on: opens its prompt window, and turns into a spinner while it's working
  • Workspaces as dots, with a springy indicator; up to six (ten in the switcher, overview and on-screen indicator), scrolling to keep yours in the middle, with the dots shrinking away at an end with more beyond
  • Before the dots, the workspace's window mode: click it for a panel to give that workspace a mode of its own (tiling, side stack, scrolling, stacking, floating or solo) or put it back to the default, and to name it
  • The focused window's icon and title (the icon can be turned off)
  • The workspace's name, off until you put it on the bar: the name you gave the workspace you're on, or "Workspace 3" without one. Click it for the window mode panel, where you can rename it

Right side

  • App tray icons, with menus styled like the dock's; optionally tucked behind one button, and optionally drawn in one colour like the rest of the bar. Icons drawn with a wide empty margin (like Spotify's) are evened up to the same size as the rest. They stay put when Slate restarts or updates
  • Notifications, keyboard layout, Bluetooth, volume, Wi-Fi and battery, each optional; optionally tucked behind one button
  • Clipboard (with how many items it holds) and screenshot buttons, off until you switch them on
  • A camera indicator, off until you put it on the bar: an open eye, lit, while an app is using your camera (hover for which), a closed one otherwise
  • Game mode button (off until you switch it on): its panel turns off glass, blur, see-through windows, shadows, animations, live window previews (the switcher and overview show each window's shape and app icon instead), notification pop-ups (they still go to the list) and ignoring the touchpad while you type, for smoother games, and puts them all back as you had them after
  • Optional values beside them: clipboard items, game mode on or off, layout code, connected devices, volume percentage, network name and battery percentage
  • Clock: 12 or 24 hour, seconds, the date (12 hour with the date, no seconds, to start with)
  • Power button

 Panels and the control centre

Control centre

  • Your profile: name, picture over the wallpaper, user and host, uptime; click the picture to change it, in a picker of its own with your picture folders, a thumbnail grid and a round preview
  • Media controls for whatever's playing
  • The time and date, with the weather beneath the calendar: today as it is now, standing out, and the next three days
  • Quick toggles: Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Caffeine (stay awake), Night light, Do not disturb, Performance mode, Airplane mode (Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and mobile broadband off together, and back as they were after), Game mode, Light mode, Mute, Mute microphone, Power saver, Glass, Charging limit, Hide the dock, Hide the menu bar, Floating windows, Clipboard history and Private Mode. Switches for hardware your computer hasn't got are left out. The panel shows eight, all the same size; put them in your own order in Settings → Control Centre, by dragging one onto another in a picture of the panel to swap them, with the ones that aren't in it waiting right beside
  • Private Mode: your microphones muted, your camera off for every app, Nova and assistants off, and screenshots and screen sharing stopped, all back as they were when you turn it off. The first time, Slate asks before adding the small helper that turns the camera off (your password, once)
  • Shortcuts to Settings and the power menu

Panels from the bar

  • Wi-Fi: networks to join, on/off. On a cable it shows a wired icon, and the panel the cable's details
  • Bluetooth: devices to connect, on/off
  • Keyboard layout: switch between your layouts
  • Sound: volume and output
  • The volume and microphone sliders show when they're in use: stripes drift along one while sound plays through it or something listens to it, and fade away when it stops
  • Battery: charge, power mode (Saver, Balanced, Performance), and the battery's health and charging limit where your laptop reports them. On a computer with no battery it's a power button, for power mode and the system readings
  • Calendar: a month view with the forecast
  • Notifications: the history
  • Clipboard: the latest things you copied, to copy again, or clear
  • Screenshot: an area, a window or the screen, and the screenshots folder
  • Power: lock, log out, restart, shut down; anything that ends the session asks first
  • Restarting can reopen your apps afterwards: a switch on the restart dialog (on by default) brings every window back on the same workspace, in the same place, then returns you to the workspace you were on

 Dock

Apps

  • Pinned and running apps, with a dot under running ones
  • Badges: an app's unread count on its icon (what it publishes itself, or its notifications since you last went to it), and a progress bar while it downloads or copies; they stay when Slate restarts
  • A hover effect: icons magnify under the pointer, or tilt (with the pointer on the dock, the icons turn in 3D towards it, and the ones near it straighten up to face you), both, fade (the icons away from the pointer fade), fade and magnify, glow (a ring and a soft glow round the icon under the pointer, in its own colour), glow and magnify, raise (just the icon under the pointer lifts up a little), or none; icon size, magnification and spread are adjustable
  • Icons bounce while their app opens
  • Always fits the screen: the icons shrink (never below 24 pixels), what's playing becomes one icon, and then the apps you haven't pinned go into More, the oldest first, a grid like Downloads' with their badges added up on it; drag one from the grid onto the dock to pin it where you drop it
  • Window previews: rest on an app to see its windows live, just above the magnified icons, and click one to go to it. A switch turns them off, and a slider sets how long to rest first, from Instant up to 3 seconds
  • Bottom, top, left or right (moving it slides it off and back in at its new edge, showing for a moment even when it auto-hides); auto-hides when a tiled window needs the room, and stays open over an empty workspace or one with only floating windows
  • With glass, the dock's background is a little clearer than the rest, with a slider for it, another for the tiles behind its icons and buttons and another for the icons themselves, and a switch for the blur behind it
  • Its corners follow your icon shape

Right-click menus

  • The app's open windows, to jump to
  • The app's own actions (a new private window, and so on)
  • New Window, Pin to Dock / Unpin from Dock
  • Drag apps: the icon comes with the pointer at its magnified size and the others part for it around a placeholder; drop a running app among the pinned ones to pin it, or pull a pinned one well up off the dock to unpin it (a warning says so first; it stays if it's running). No previews or menus while dragging
  • Drop files on an app to open them in it, or on the Waste Basket to bin them
  • Close Window, or Close 2 Windows
  • Force Quit, for an app that has stopped responding
  • Right-click the dock's empty space: Auto-hide, Position, Hover effect, Icon size (small, medium, large) and Show (the Apps button, what's playing, Downloads, the waste basket), each a submenu with the current choice ticked, and a link to its settings
  • Centred on the icon, with a pointer to it
  • Every menu works from the keys too: Up and Down to move, Enter to choose, Right and Left in and out of a submenu

Media, Downloads and the Waste Basket

  • What's playing, after the Apps button: its album art (optional) with Previous, Play/Pause and Next beside it and a progress bar beneath (over the art, under the pointer, on a side dock), and a right-click menu with them all
  • A Downloads stack: click for a grid of your newest downloads (pictures as thumbnails), right-click to open the folder
  • Dividers after the Apps button and media controls, between pinned apps and the others running, and before Downloads; the Apps button can be switched off
  • A waste basket at the end, filled when there's something in it: click to open it, right-click to empty it (it asks first)
  • Both can be switched off in Settings → Dock

 Launcher

Find anything

  • Tap Super on its own, or Super+Space, and type
  • Apps sorted by how often you use them (optional)
  • Every setting in Settings is searchable from here
  • Does sums as you type, and conversions: "5 km in miles", "72 f to c", "today + 30 days"
  • Forgiving: initials and small slips still find apps ("vsc", "fierfox")
  • Open windows by title, apps' own actions (New Private Window…), recent files and folders
  • Quick toggles: Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, airplane mode, Private Mode, Do Not Disturb, night light and game mode
  • "> command" runs a command; ":name" finds an emoji; a last result searches the web
  • The apps you opened most recently first, before you type
  • Each of these can be switched off in Settings → Launcher
  • Shrinks smoothly to fit the results as you type, with the search box staying put
  • Open an app on a new workspace at the end, or to the left or the right, with a key

Layout

  • A list, with or without descriptions
  • Or a grid of icons: columns and rows are adjustable, icons scale to fit, and the name shows at the bottom

 Windows

Tiling or traditional

  • Windows tile themselves by default
  • Each workspace can have its own window mode, from the menu bar; Settings → Windows sets the default for the rest, and spaces remember each workspace's mode
  • Name a workspace ("Coding", "Music") with the pencil on that panel: the name takes the place of "Workspace 3" in the workspace switcher, the bar's hover text and the Spaces panel, stays for as long as the workspace is around, and is saved and restored with a Space
  • Changing the default with windows open asks first: tidy up, moving every workspace (those with a mode of their own too) to the new mode, or leave them as they are and use it only for new workspaces
  • Or pick another of Hyprland's layouts from Settings → Windows: side stack (one main window, the rest stacked beside it), scrolling (columns on a strip that scrolls sideways) stacking (one window at a time, filling the screen) or solo (every window fills a workspace of its own; a new one opens on the next free workspace)
  • Or floating windows with title bars: drag to move, close and maximise buttons, double-click to maximise and click again to restore
  • A floating window dropped with its top above or under the menu bar slides back down below it, title bar and all, so it can always be grabbed again
  • Slate Bars, Slate's own title bars, by default, with nothing to install or rebuild: a bar on the floating window in front that wraps round its rounded corners; dragging it moves the window by a handle in its middle, and its top corners resize it; maximising keeps the bar; windows take focus when clicked
  • Optional: Hyprbars, a Hyprland plugin with a title bar on every floating window. Choose it in Settings → Windows and Slate explains what's involved, builds it in the background and switches once it's ready
  • After a Hyprland update, when Hyprbars can't load until it's rebuilt: no error messages, Slate Bars take over, and Slate asks whether to rebuild, offers a reload when it's done, then asks whether to switch back. Rebuild, Retry, Reload and Remove are in Settings → Windows
  • Float or tile any window with a key
  • Maximise, full screen, split the other way, resize with the arrow keys
  • Move and resize with Super and the mouse

Look and feel

  • Window borders, spacing between windows, and space around a lone window (3 px borders and 6 px spacing to start with)
  • Dim inactive windows, 50% to start with (Settings itself never dims, so you can watch a change take effect)
  • Focus follows the pointer (optional)
  • Shadows under everything raised (floating windows, panels, menus, the launcher, notifications), one close, crisp look everywhere, dark with a light tint of your theme, with glass on too; in Settings → Appearance, turn them off, or set their darkness, softness, spread, distance and direction, for every shadow alike
  • Picture-in-picture videos float and stay on top
  • Games (Steam and gamescope) go full screen on their own workspace
  • Terminals step aside for the apps they open

Switcher

  • Super+Tab: every window in a circular strip, showing live previews, app icons (each window's outline with its icon, nothing captured) or both
  • Alt+Tab for the next window
  • Choose how it moves: the strip slides along, hops along, turns like a wheel with the windows round its edge, tilts the others away in 3D, rides round a 3D ring (orbit), deals from a pile (deck), spreads like a hand of cards (fan), or jumps straight there; and how it goes to the window you pick: a crossfade, your workspace animation, or instantly

Spaces

  • Save the apps on one or more workspaces, and where their windows sit, as a space, from the Spaces button on the bar
  • Pick the workspaces from small live previews of them, then give the space a name, an icon and a colour (pastels, or any colour of your own)
  • Click a space to bring it back: each workspace opens as a new one, its apps start, and their windows go back to the same tiles, floating spots and sizes
  • Windows opened from a space get a thin top border in its colour and wear its icon in their top left corner; the badge fades across to the other top corner when the pointer comes near, the window's icon on the menu bar sits on the space's colour, and the badge shows on the window in the window switcher and the workspace overview (each can be switched off in Settings → Spaces)
  • Saving under a name you already use replaces that space; delete one from its row (it asks first, in the middle of the screen)
  • Settings → Spaces: the button on the menu bar, and each way their windows are marked
  • slate ipc call spaces restore <name> brings one back from a key binding or script

 Workspaces

Overview

  • Super+Down: every workspace as a large card, its windows showing live previews, app icons (each window's outline with its icon, nothing captured) or both
  • Jump to one, or hold Shift to lift a card and move it along: its windows (laid out as they were), name and window mode go with it
  • Card size is adjustable (or, as a grid, how many go across)
  • Choose how it moves: the row slides along, hops along, turns like a wheel, tilts the others away in 3D, rides round a 3D ring, deals from a pile, spreads like a hand of cards, or jumps straight there, or lay every workspace out in a grid (2 × 2 up to 9 × 9, scrolling when there are more, with the arrow keys moving round it); and how it goes to the workspace you pick: zooming into its card, fading away, your workspace animation, or instantly

Moving around

  • Next and previous workspace, with an on-screen indicator that names the workspace you land on (a switch turns the name off), wrapping round from the last to the first (optional; off, the overview's row ends there too)
  • Go to, or move a window to, workspace 1–9 (per monitor too)
  • Move a window to a new workspace after the last one in use
  • Always keep an empty workspace, last in the row (optional)
  • A secret workspace, to show or hide over the others; a note in the middle says when it's empty
  • Follows apps you're sent to: a link opened from another app takes you to the browser

 Notifications and indicators

Notifications

  • Slate's own notification server
  • Pop-ups: position, how long they stay, and how many at once
  • A history to catch up on, and clear
  • Do not disturb
  • A soft chime when one pops up (optional)
  • Shown on your main display

On-screen indicators

  • Volume, microphone, brightness and keyboard backlight
  • The camera: "Camera on", with the app using it, the moment one starts, and "Camera off" when it's let go
  • The workspace dots when you switch

 Desktop

Wallpaper

  • Pick from a folder of your own, shown as thumbnails in Settings
  • Ten wallpapers of Slate's own, in 4K, soft glows of colour over a deep base, each with Slate's mark in the middle (see-through, in the wallpaper's colours) or plain: Ember, Iris, Lagoon and Blossom in mixed colours, then Fern (green), Cobalt (blue), Ruby (red), Honey (yellow), Graphite (black and grey) and Mist (white and grey). They're listed below your own pictures (a switch hides them)
  • Crossfades when it changes
  • Changes by itself on a timer or at each login, in a shuffled order (optional)
  • Leaves out small pictures (icons, avatars), with a minimum size

Right-click menu

  • App launcher, Terminal, Files
  • Change wallpaper, Next wallpaper
  • Display settings, Settings
  • One menu everywhere: the desktop's, the menu bar's and the dock's look and behave alike, point at what they're for (the desktop's at the cursor), open submenus to the side, and close when you change workspace or open the launcher, a switcher or Nova

Hot corners

  • Each corner can lock the screen, open the launcher, the workspace overview, the control centre or notifications, or turn the screen off
  • An adjustable delay; disarmed while locked; none are set to start with
  • Set on a picture of the screen in Settings, showing what each corner does

Night light

  • A warmer screen in the evening, with adjustable warmth
  • On by hand, sunset to sunrise for your location, or a custom schedule

 Appearance

Theme and colour

  • Dark, light, or automatic by sunset (light mode is still being polished, so Slate asks before switching to it)
  • Accent colour from a preset, any colour you like, or the wallpaper (optional), with an Accent boldness slider for how vivid the wallpaper's colour is
  • Colourful (the default: soft pastels, such as each Settings page in its own colour, which settings found in the launcher and Slate's notifications share) or Monotone (the accent and greys)
  • Buttons, rows and menu items light up in the accent colour as you point at them
  • Presets that set the theme, colours, glass and folder colours (from the wallpaper) in one go (asks first; your wallpaper stays): Slate Default (back to how Slate starts out), Liquid Glass and Metro Flat
  • Background tint from the wallpaper (optional), with a Tint boldness slider for how much colour it adds
  • Icons: any icon theme installed, for your apps (GTK, Qt and KDE alike) and Slate's own dock, launcher and bar, which redraw themselves in it. Kora, MoreWaita, Numix Circle, Zafiro and Nordzy go in with one click, no password needed
  • Folder colour for file managers: from the wallpaper, the theme's own, or any colour your icons come in. Papirus, Kora and Nordzy, with Desktop, Home and the other special folders in the same colour
  • Everything (bar, panels, Settings, themed apps) shifts together when the wallpaper changes

Style

  • Rounded screen corners
  • Rounded UI elements: switch them off and everything Slate draws, and your windows, goes hard-edged and square
  • Workspace indicators as circles or squares
  • App icons as tiles, squircles, circles, squares or their original shape, filled or not, optionally tinted with the accent; tiles in the theme's colour or a deep shade of each icon's own

Fonts, size and motion

  • Interface font (Geist by default) and code font (Geist Mono by default)
  • Interface size, 75–150%: sizes Slate itself on every display, on top of each display's scale, leaving apps as they are (asks before restarting Slate to apply it)
  • Glass: the bar, dock, panels, menus, notifications, Settings and the lock and login screens turn see-through with the desktop blurred behind (the menu bar a little darker in dark mode, so it reads as a steady band), with opacity, blur, shine and gloss to adjust, and window borders get a sheen across their top-left corner (off by default). App icons sit on solid, glossy tiles everywhere: the dock, launcher, switchers and notifications
  • Notifications have glass settings of their own: blur on or off, how solid they are, and how solid while the pointer's on one
  • So do the launcher and Nova's window: blur on or off, and how solid each is
  • Fast blur (behind see-through windows and glass, the wallpaper only, for smooth animation; on by default) and a blur quality setting (Medium by default)
  • Animations: normal, faster or off, for windows and workspaces and for Slate's own panels, fades and highlights alike
  • Workspace switching: Slide Fade, Slide, Crossfade, vertical slides or none
  • While a switch slides, the workspace you're leaving stays solid until the new one has covered it, and windows go square at the corners, so the desktop never shows between them

 App theming

Opt-in, app by app, in Settings → App theming. Each app you switch on follows Slate's colours, light or dark, and the accent, with text colours kept readable against the background (in light mode too). Slate backs up a config before it first changes it, and switching an app off puts it back the way it was, keeping your own edits. Flatpak builds are themed too, in their own folders. Each app says what it is and links to its website, and one that isn't installed can be installed from there, from your distribution's own software (it asks first, then for your password, and a notification says when it's done).

 Displays

Monitors

  • Arrange them by dragging
  • Turn each on or off; resolution, refresh rate, scale and rotation per monitor
  • HDR for monitors that support it: Auto (while a game or video fills the screen with HDR), On (always, in 10-bit colour, with how bright and colourful everything else looks) or Off
  • Choose the main display, where notifications appear
  • Changes revert by themselves unless you keep them

 Keyboard, mouse and touchpad

Keyboard

  • Several layouts, with a shortcut to switch between them and a menu bar item
  • Caps Lock as itself, another Ctrl, Escape, nothing, or the layout switcher
  • Repeat delay and speed (500 ms and 16 a second to start with)

Mouse

  • Pointer speed (for mice and touchpads) and acceleration
  • Natural scrolling
  • Pointer style: Slate's own cursor, or any cursor theme installed, for Hyprland and every app alike. A few good ones (Phinger, Apple Cursor, Bibata, Nordzy, Rosé Pine, Catppuccin) go in with one click, no password needed
  • Pointer size
  • The pointer becomes a hand over buttons, switches and links in Slate's panels and windows; it stays an arrow over the desktop, the dock and right-click menus

Touchpad

  • Scroll speed
  • Tap to click
  • Ignore while typing

 Sound

Output and input

  • Choose the speakers or headphones, volume, mute
  • Choose the microphone, input level, mute

Sound effects

  • Each with its own volume slider, from off up
  • A tick when you change the volume
  • A soft chime with notifications
  • A gentle, questioning sound for confirmations, password prompts and urgent notifications
  • A soft, warm tone when Nova answers while you're waiting with its window open
  • A barely-there tap on buttons and menus
  • A soft swish switching workspaces and windows, drifting the way you go
  • A gentle welcome when you log in, as Slate fades in from the dark behind its logo, and a closing sound as it fades to black when you log out, restart or shut down

 Power

Lid and power button

  • Closing the lid: suspend, lock, turn the screen off, or nothing
  • Power button: show the power menu, suspend, shut down (asks first), lock, or nothing

Power mode

  • Saver, Balanced or Performance, chosen separately for on battery and plugged in
  • A charging limit, where your laptop has one: stop short of full to keep the battery healthy, with a question before charging to full again

Restarting

  • Reopen apps after restarting: each window comes back on the same workspace and screen, tiled or floating just as it was (also a switch on the restart dialog)

 Lock and login

Lock screen

  • Your wallpaper and picture, and your account password
  • Crossfades in over your desktop and back out after your password, while staying fully locked throughout (no fade before sleep)
  • A countdown after too many tries
  • A power menu on the lock screen (optional)
  • Lock before sleep

When you're away

  • Lock, turn the screen off, and suspend after times you choose
  • Waits while media plays

Screensaver

  • Your wallpaper (dimmed and slowly drifting), a slideshow of your wallpaper folder, videos, or black, each with or without the clock (on black it moves now and then so nothing burns in)
  • Videos: a folder of your own, silent and fading from one to the next, or twelve free scenery clips to download from Settings (Earth from the space station, an aurora, clouds over a canyon, a waterfall, Lake Bled at sunrise and from the air, South Georgia's beaches and penguins, an emperor penguin in Antarctica, the Crimean coast, the Blue Ridge mountains and a beach in Cádiz), in up to 4K to suit your screen; needs Qt Multimedia
  • Starts after a time you choose, before the lock; any key, click or pointer move puts it away
  • Lock on wake (optional): waking it brings up the lock screen
  • Settings → Screensaver, with a preview

Login screen

  • A matching greetd login screen, with your wallpaper and picture
  • Optional: nothing changes your login screen until you choose Install in Settings → Lock & Idle, which first says what's involved (it replaces the system's login screen and needs updating with your password when Slate, Hyprland, Quickshell or greetd change)
  • Switch back to your old login screen from the same place
  • Follows your wallpaper, theme and picture automatically; a new design asks for your password once, with an urgent notification that stays until you answer it (only for changes to the login screen itself; "Later" waits a week)
  • Resync from Settings → Debug, if you changed something outside Slate
  • Safe updates: each one is test-loaded before it's installed, and if the login screen ever fails at boot, the last version that worked takes over, then a basic emergency login

 Apps, startup and time

Default apps

  • Terminal, web browser, files, text editor and calculator, used by the shortcuts and menus

Startup apps

  • Apps to open at each login
  • Removing one asks first
  • After a restart that reopens your apps, each opens on a workspace of its own, so they don't crowd the reopened ones

Date, time and location

  • Time zone, and setting the time automatically
  • Date format (day first, month first or year first), the week's first day, and Celsius or Fahrenheit
  • Your location, for the weather and sunset times: places come up as you type, to pick from a list

 Nova, the assistant

Ask for what you want in a line, and an AI agent you already use does it through Slate's own tools. It's off until you switch it on, and switching it on tells you what that means first.

Ask Nova

  • Super+K, the button on the menu bar, or your keyboard's Copilot or assistant key opens a small prompt window, like the launcher
  • Type what you want: "turn on glass", "put Firefox on workspace 2", "make the dock smaller", "where do I change the font?"
  • The answer appears as it's written, with a list of what was done, in Slate's own words, and a soft, warm tone when it's done if you've been waiting (quiet in game mode)
  • Answers can carry steps as a list, bold words and commands in a box with a Copy button. Pictures and links are shown as words, never loaded or opened by themselves
  • It reads like a chat: your lines on the right, Nova's on the left, each with when it was said
  • What you type within a few minutes follows on from the last answer; there are no conversations to manage
  • Your chat history is still there after a restart (the last 100 messages, kept on your computer only), unless you switch that off
  • Close the window and it carries on, with a notification when it's done
  • Stop ends it at any time

Your own agent

  • Nova uses an agent you already have and are signed in to: Claude Code, or a command of your own. Slate keeps no keys and sends nothing anywhere itself
  • You choose the agent: there's none to start with, and Nova asks you to pick one in Settings before it does anything. What the one you pick needs shows beneath it
  • With Claude Code, Slate switches off the agent's own tools for the prompt, so it works through Slate's alone
  • An agent Slate can't limit that way is marked "Keeps its own tools", and choosing it asks first
  • Slate tells the agent who it is here and a few lines about the moment; Settings shows you exactly what

What Slate asks you first

  • Changing a setting, arranging the menu bar, moving a window, opening an app or a Space, the wallpaper, volume and brightness happen straight away, and can be put back
  • Closing a window, deleting a Space, restoring a Settings page's defaults, and logging out, restarting, shutting down or sleeping always ask, in Slate's own confirmation window
  • A switch makes Slate ask about every change
  • Some settings are yours alone: what the assistant may do, locking, keyboard layouts, default and startup apps, folders and your location
  • Everything it did is listed in Settings → Assistant → Activity

What it's told, and what it isn't

  • What you type, and what Slate tells the assistant about your desktop (your settings, your open windows and their titles), goes to the service behind your agent, under your own account with them
  • Slate's tools answer in words: they never show an assistant your screen, your files or your clipboard
  • Window titles and notifications each have a switch; notifications start off
  • Slate does its best to keep an assistant within these limits, but it can't promise what one will do, so using it is your choice and your responsibility

 Built-in tools

Screenshots

  • An area or a window (the screen freezes while you pick), or the whole screen
  • Saved to your chosen folder with your chosen names, and copied
  • Then optionally opened in swash to mark up; saving there updates the same file

Clipboard, emoji and colours

  • Clipboard history, text and images (Super+V), sized to what's in it; passwords are never kept, and history can be switched off
  • An emoji picker (Super+.)
  • Pick a colour from anywhere on screen (Super+P), copied
  • Zoom in and out of the screen

Printers and scanners

  • Your printers with what they're doing and the jobs waiting; make one the default, print a test page, cancel jobs or remove one
  • Find printers on your network or plugged in by USB and add them in one click, with no driver to install (most from the last ten years)
  • Any other printer through the printer settings app (or CUPS's own page)
  • Scanners found, and a button to scan with your scanning app

Webcam

  • A preview, only when you ask for it (the camera's light comes on only then), and which apps are using the camera
  • Brightness, contrast, colour, sharpness, gain and gamma; automatic or manual white balance and exposure; anti-flicker for your mains (50 or 60 Hz); backlight compensation
  • Kept: webcams forget their settings when unplugged or restarted, so Slate puts yours back at login

 The Settings app

Finding things

  • Opens on All Settings: a tile for every page, with a search across all settings; every other page has the same search at its top
  • A Debug page (from All Settings): reload everything (Hyprland's config and Slate itself, your apps stay open), resync the login screen, set how much detail goes in Slate's log (asks before restarting Slate), export recent logs with system details to attach to a bug report (your location is left out), and factory reset Slate: it asks twice, can clear your own Hyprland tweaks and QML too, and keeps everything in a backup folder first
  • Pages: General, Appearance (with Wallpaper, Icons and App Theming a click away at its top), Wallpaper, Icons, Displays, Input, Sound, Power, Windows, Workspaces, Notifications, Network, Bluetooth, Printers, Webcam, Menu Bar, Control Centre, Dock, Launcher, Spaces, Hot Corners, Security, Shortcuts, Default Apps, Startup Apps, Region, Screenshots, App Theming, Assistant, Updates, Debug, About
  • Restore defaults on each page, and a small dot after any setting you've changed: point at it to see the default
  • Move it by dragging its sidebar top or page header
  • A back button on every page takes you to All Settings
  • Comes back on the same page, scrolled to the same place, if Slate reloads or restarts while it's open
  • Reloading is gentle: the screen fades to "Reloading Slate" with a spinner while the new one starts, then fades back. Your apps and windows stay open

Updates

  • Checks for a new version daily, with a notification when one's out
  • What's new, and updating in one click, keeping your settings and tweaks
  • Keeps the version before, so you can go back to it
  • A version that needs new packages, or changes the login session, installs them as it updates, after asking for your password; if one goes missing later, a notification offers to put it back

Shortcuts page

  • Every keyboard shortcut, searchable, including ones you add to your own Hyprland config
  • Three sets to choose from: Slate's, Hyprland's defaults, or Hyprland's with Slate's added where the keys are free

Wi-Fi and Bluetooth

  • Networks to join, with passwords
  • Devices to connect, and finding new ones

 Under the hood

Built on

  • Hyprland 0.57 or newer, with its Lua config
  • Quickshell 0.3 or newer (QML)
  • Geist and Tabler icons, bundled

Scriptable

  • Everything is reachable from a terminal: slate ipc call settings page displays, … launcher open, … wallpaper set <file> and more
  • Settings live in one JSON file
  • An MCP server, slate mcp: add it to an AI agent you already use and, once you switch it on in Settings → Assistant (which tells you what it means first), you can ask that agent to change a setting, move a window, open a Space or tell you what's where. Slate asks you before anything that can't be undone, like closing a window or shutting down, and its tools answer in words: Slate never shows them your screen, your files or your clipboard. It's off until you say so, and what an assistant does is yours to decide: Slate does its best to keep one in bounds, but can't promise what it will do. Settings → Assistant also has what it may see (window titles, notifications), a switch to be asked about every change, and a list of everything assistants did
  • Your own tweaks in ~/.config/slate: Hyprland files for environment, apps, monitors, shortcuts, window rules, startup commands and anything else, and a QML file loaded into Slate, each made with instructions in it, kept on updates, and reloaded as you save (mistakes show in a notification)
  • Slate reloads as its files change; if a change has a mistake, a notification says what and where, Slate keeps running as it was, and reloads as soon as you save the fix

Installs on

  • Arch, Debian and Ubuntu, Fedora, openSUSE and NixOS, with one installer; and, new, without systemd on Artix, Void and Gentoo
  • An early preview: you may run into bugs. Please report them in a GitHub issue
