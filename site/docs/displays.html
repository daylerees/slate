Your computer

Displays

Slate picks up every screen you plug in and gives each one its own menu bar. When you'd like things a little different, everything about your screens is in one place, and nothing you change sticks until you say so.

Arranging your screens

Open  Settings → Displays. With more than one screen connected, the top of the page shows them as tiles, to scale, laid out as they are now. Drag a tile beside another to match where the screen really is, so the pointer crosses from one to the next where you'd expect. The main display is marked Main.

Click a tile to choose that screen. Its settings appear underneath, headed with its name.

A screen's settings

Until you choose otherwise, each screen starts at its native resolution and the fastest refresh rate it has there, never slower than the screen itself recommends. Some screens recommend a smaller mode than they can show (a super-wide monitor at a quarter of its size, say), so Slate doesn't simply take the screen's word for it. If the fastest rate doesn't work with your cable or graphics card, Slate tries the next one. Anything you choose here, or set in your own monitors.lua, is left as you have it.

SettingWhat it does
Use this displayTurn a screen off to leave it dark and unused. The only screen in use can't be turned off, so you're never left without one.
ResolutionThe sizes the screen reports, with its native one marked. Laptop panels often offer only their own, so to make things larger or smaller there, use Scale instead.
Refresh rateHow many times a second the picture updates, from the rates this resolution has. Higher is smoother.
ScaleHow large everything on this screen looks, apps included. Only scales that suit the resolution are offered.
RotationFor a screen turned on its side or upside down.
HDROnly for screens that support it. Auto uses HDR while a game or video fills the screen with HDR content, On keeps it on all the time in 10-bit color, and Off never uses it.
SDR brightness and SDR colorWith HDR on, how bright and how colorful everything that isn't HDR looks: most apps, and the desktop.
Main displayWhere the main menu bar lives. Every screen has a bar, and the launcher and other panels open on the screen you're using.

When you pick a new resolution, Slate keeps the refresh rate if the new size has it (or takes its fastest), and moves the scale to the nearest one that suits it.

Keeping a change

A new resolution or refresh rate can leave a screen blank if it doesn't really suit it. So whenever you change something, Slate asks Keep these display settings? in the middle of the screen, and at the top of the page with a countdown. Choose Keep if everything looks right, or Go back to undo it straight away. If you can't see the question at all, just wait: when the countdown runs out, Slate puts things back as they were by itself. Closing Settings (Esc does it) or going to another page without choosing puts them back straight away too.

Making Slate bigger or smaller

A screen's scale changes everything on it, your apps included. If you'd rather make just Slate larger or smaller (the bar, the dock, the panels and the Settings window) on every screen at once, use Interface size in  Settings → Appearance. It works on top of each screen's scale. See Appearance.

Sharp older apps

At a scale that isn't a whole number, like 125%, apps made for older Linux desktops (X11), like Steam, can look blurry: they're drawn at 100% and stretched. Turn on Sharp older apps in  Settings → Displays → Older apps and they draw at your display's own pixels and scale themselves to your main display's scale, so they're sharp. A few that can't scale themselves look smaller. Apps you open after changing it follow it, so restart Steam if it's open. Apps other than Steam are told the scale through xrdb, if it's installed.

Brightness

Your keyboard's brightness keys change the screen's brightness (and the keyboard's backlight, where it has keys for it), with a small pop-up showing the new level, just like the volume.

Each display also has a Brightness slider in  Settings → Displays, with its other settings: choose the display in the arrangement first. On a laptop's own screen it's the same brightness the keys change, and it moves with them while Settings is open.

For a monitor, Slate sets the brightness through the monitor's own controls (DDC/CI), so it changes the backlight itself rather than dimming the picture. It can take a moment to change, and Slate sets it once you stop dragging. That needs a couple of things:

  • ddcutil installed. Until it is, the slider is grayed out and says so.
  • Access to the monitors' I2C connection. If the row says Needs access to i2c, load the i2c-dev module (sudo modprobe i2c-dev, and add it to /etc/modules-load.d/ to keep it) and add yourself to the i2c group (sudo usermod -aG i2c $USER, then log out and back in). Some distributions give you access with ddcutil's own udev rule instead. Slate never changes this for you.
  • DDC/CI switched on in the monitor's own menu. A monitor that doesn't answer says This display doesn't let Slate change its brightness; its own buttons still work as always.

Automatic brightness

On a laptop with a light sensor, the Brightness section of  Settings → Displays has an Automatic brightness switch (off to start with). With it on, your screen follows the light in the room, and so does your keyboard's backlight where it has one: brighter in daylight, dimmer in the evening, and the keyboard's light on in the dark. Changes are slow and gentle, and a passing shadow or a flicker isn't followed.

It learns from you. Whenever you change the brightness yourself, Slate remembers what you like for that light, waits a minute before following the room again, and from then on uses your choice in that light. What it has learned stays on your computer, readable by you alone.

The switch only appears where there's a sensor. If your laptop has one but the switch is grayed out, install iio-sensor-proxy, the system service that reads it. Automatic brightness keeps working in game mode.

Your own monitor rules

If you'd rather set your screens up by hand, rules in ~/.config/slate/hypr/monitors.lua take priority over the Displays page. Slate never overwrites that file. See Your own config.
